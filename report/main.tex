\begin{abstract}
The choice of at-most-one (AMO) encoding changes the size and propagation behavior of a Boolean satisfiability (SAT) formula for N-Queens. An archived benchmark has unequal method coverage, so it cannot compare all five encodings at large sizes. This study implements pairwise, binary, commander, sequential counter, and product encodings for empty-board N-Queens and compares them with integer linear programming and constraint programming models. Two clean-source runs test the five SAT encodings, CBC, Gurobi, and CP-SAT at $N\in\{20,50,100,150,200\}$, with three repetitions and a 120-s full-process deadline per case. At $N=200$, median construction-plus-solver-call time is 2.252~s for Commander/Glucose4, 0.421~s for Gurobi, and 44.776~s for CP-SAT. Sequential and CBC time out in all three repetitions. Exact formula counts and unit-propagation tests check the implemented encodings. The results describe one machine and five feasible board sizes; they do not establish general solver superiority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The N-Queens problem asks for $N$ non-attacking queens on an $N\times N$ board. Direct constructions find a placement for $N=1$ and every $N\geq4$ in polynomial time \cite{bell2009survey}. The empty-board problem still permits a controlled comparison of SAT, integer linear programming (ILP), and constraint programming (CP) formulations. Its results do not extend automatically to N-Queens Completion or unrelated combinatorial problems \cite{gent2017complexity}.

SAT solvers use conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL), but they require a conjunctive normal form (CNF) representation of the placement constraints \cite{marques2009cdcl}. Different at-most-one (AMO) encodings can change both CNF size and what unit propagation deduces \cite{prestwich2007cnf}. The archived benchmark includes only the binary encoding at $N=150$ and $N=200$. It therefore cannot compare all five SAT encodings at those sizes.

This study asks how the five encodings differ in formula size and measured runtime under a common process limit. It also compares SAT with ILP and CP-SAT on the same empty-board sizes. The paper checks exact CNF counts and AMO propagation, then reports archived and clean-source measurements separately. The study does not propose a new encoding or claim a general solver ranking.

\section{Background and related work}

\subsection{The N-Queens problem}
A solution places one queen in each row and column and at most one on each diagonal. Empty boards with $N=2$ or $N=3$ have no solution. N-Queens Completion adds pre-placed queens; its decision and counting versions are NP-complete and \#P-complete, respectively \cite{gent2017complexity}. This study seeks one placement on an empty board.

\subsection{Boolean satisfiability (SAT)}
SAT asks whether a Boolean formula has a satisfying assignment. In CNF, the formula is a conjunction of clauses, and each clause is a disjunction of literals.

Modern SAT solvers extend the Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland (DPLL) procedure with CDCL \cite{marques1999grasp,marques2009cdcl,moskewicz2001chaff}. Glucose uses literal block distance (LBD), the number of decision levels in a learned clause, to assess learned clauses \cite{audemard2009predicting,audemard2018glucose}. The SAT experiments use Glucose4 through PySAT.

\subsection{Cardinality constraints}
Cardinality constraints limit the number of true variables in $X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}$. The N-Queens model uses three forms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At least one (ALO):} $\sum x_i \geq 1$, encoded by the clause $(x_1 \vee x_2 \vee \dots \vee x_n)$.
    \item \textbf{At most one (AMO):} $\sum x_i \leq 1$. Several CNF encodings trade formula size against propagation strength \cite{frisch2005sat}.
    \item \textbf{Exactly one (EXO):} $\sum x_i = 1$, equivalent to ALO $\wedge$ AMO.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integer linear programming (ILP)}
ILP uses linear constraints and integer-valued decision variables \cite{nemhauser1988integer}. Solvers can combine linear relaxations with branch-and-bound and cutting planes. The baselines use Gurobi \cite{gurobi}, IBM ILOG CPLEX \cite{cplex}, and CBC through PuLP \cite{mitchell1996lp}.

\subsection{Constraint programming (CP)}
CP searches for assignments that satisfy stated constraints. Global constraints such as \texttt{AllDifferent} can use specialized filtering, including Régin's matching-based method \cite{regin1994filtering,rossi2006handbook}. The baselines use OR-Tools CP-SAT \cite{ortools} and IBM ILOG CPLEX CP Optimizer \cite{cplex}. Their internal propagation cannot be determined from the model alone.

\subsection{Related work}
Prestwich reviewed CNF translations of constraint satisfaction problems \cite{prestwich2007cnf}. Pairwise AMO uses $O(n^2)$ clauses; Sinz's sequential counter reduces this to $O(n)$ clauses and auxiliary variables \cite{sinz2005towards}. Klieber and Kwon proposed hierarchical commander groups \cite{klieber2007efficient}, while Chen studied grid and product encodings \cite{chen2010new}. All five encodings in this paper are established methods.

\section{SAT encodings for N-Queens}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
For each cell $(i,j)$ of an $N\times N$ board, let $x_{i,j}$ be true exactly when that cell contains a queen. Here $i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$.

The board-level constraint has six components. For each encoding $E$, auxiliary variables $Y$ are allocated separately for each AMO set, and the resulting CNF $\Phi_E(X,Y)$ satisfies $\Phi_{\mathrm{board}}(X)\iff\exists Y\,\Phi_E(X,Y)$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \Phi^{row}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{row}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{diag}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{anti}_{AMO}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Row and column constraints}
Every row and column must contain exactly one queen. For row $i$, the ALO clause is
\begin{equation}
\bigvee_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j}
\end{equation}
The chosen encoding supplies row AMO clauses; columns use the same construction. Each row and column therefore satisfies EXO = ALO $\wedge$ AMO.

\subsection{Diagonal constraints}
Cells share a main diagonal when $d=i-j$ is equal, with $d\in\{-(N-1),\dots,N-1\}$. They share an anti-diagonal when $s=i+j$ is equal, with $s\in\{2,\dots,2N\}$. Each diagonal needs AMO but may be empty, so it needs no ALO clause.

\subsection{AMO encoding schemes}
For $n$ Boolean variables $X=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$, AMO permits at most one true input:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \leq 1
\end{equation}
The five implementations encode this constraint in CNF with different auxiliary-variable and clause counts. All five propagate a true input to the falsity of every other AMO input.

\subsubsection{Binomial (pairwise) encoding}
The pairwise encoding forbids every pair of inputs from being true together:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binomial}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
\end{equation}
It uses no auxiliary variables and $\binom{n}{2}=O(n^2)$ binary clauses per AMO constraint. Across the board, the clause count is $O(N^3)$. If $x_i$ is true, unit propagation falsifies every other input $x_j$. This is generalized arc consistency (GAC) on the original AMO inputs.

\subsubsection{Binary encoding}
Binary encoding assigns each input a code over $m=\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ auxiliary bits $Y=\{y_0,\dots,y_{m-1}\}$ \cite{frisch2005sat}. Each index $k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ has a unique expansion $k=\sum_{b=0}^{m-1}\text{bit}(k,b)2^b$.

The literal required by bit $b$ of code $k$ is
\begin{equation}
\ell(k, b) = \begin{cases}
y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 1, \\
\neg y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The binary AMO formula is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binary}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \bigwedge_{b=0}^{m-1} (\neg x_k \vee \ell(k, b))
\end{equation}
If $x_k$ is true, unit propagation fixes every bit in $Y$. Each other input requires a different bit in at least one position, so its corresponding clause propagates $\neg x_j$. This establishes GAC on the original AMO inputs, not propagation completeness for arbitrary auxiliary assignments. The encoding uses $\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ auxiliary variables and $n\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ binary clauses per constraint.

\subsubsection{Commander encoding}
Commander encoding partitions $X$ into groups $G_1,\dots,G_m$ of at most three inputs and applies the construction recursively \cite{klieber2007efficient}. Each group has one auxiliary commander variable $c_k$.

The encoding has four clause sets:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Within-group AMO ($\Phi_{\text{intra}}$):} Forbid two true inputs in one group:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{intra}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{\substack{x_i, x_j \in G_k \\ i < j}} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Forward implication ($\Phi_{\text{fwd}}$):} A true group input forces its commander true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{fwd}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{x \in G_k} (\neg x \vee c_k)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Reverse implication ($\Phi_{\text{rev}}$):} A true commander requires a true group input:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{rev}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \left( \neg c_k \vee \bigvee_{x \in G_k} x \right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Commander AMO ($\Phi_{\text{cmd}}$):} Apply AMO recursively to the commanders:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{cmd}} = \text{AMO}(\{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_m\})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The recursion terminates at $n\leq3$ with pairwise AMO. The implementation includes reverse implications, so $c_k\leftrightarrow\bigvee_{x\in G_k}x$. Reverse clauses are optional for the projected AMO relation and input GAC. Forward implications, within-group AMO, and recursive commander AMO already propagate $\neg x_j$ after $x_i$ becomes true \cite{klieber2007efficient}. The implemented version uses $O(n)$ auxiliary variables and clauses.

\subsubsection{Sequential counter encoding}
Sinz's sequential counter uses $n-1$ auxiliary variables $s_1,\dots,s_{n-1}$ \cite{sinz2005towards}. They carry the prefix implications needed to reject a second true input. The clauses do not force each $s_i$ to equal the prefix disjunction in every satisfying auxiliary assignment.

The clauses enforce prefix propagation and collision prohibition:
\begin{align}
& (\neg x_1 \vee s_1) \\
& \bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_i \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg s_{i-1} \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg x_i \vee \neg s_{i-1}) \right) \\
& (\neg x_n \vee \neg s_{n-1})
\end{align}
The encoding uses $n-1=O(n)$ auxiliary variables and $3n-4=O(n)$ clauses per constraint. If $x_i$ is true, unit propagation follows the prefix chain to falsify later inputs and propagates backward to falsify earlier inputs. It is GAC on the original AMO inputs.

\subsubsection{Product encoding}
Product encoding places the $n$ inputs on a two-dimensional grid \cite{chen2010new}. It sets $p=\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ and $q=\lceil n/p\rceil$, so $n\leq pq$. The auxiliary coordinates are $U=\{u_1,\dots,u_p\}$ and $V=\{v_1,\dots,v_q\}$.

Each variable $x_k$ ($k \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$) is mapped to grid coordinates $r = \lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1$ and $c = (k \bmod q) + 1$ via channeling clauses:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{chan}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_k \vee u_{\lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1}) \wedge (\neg x_k \vee v_{(k \bmod q) + 1}) \right)
\end{equation}
Pairwise AMO constraints are then enforced independently across $U$ and $V$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{grid}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq p} (\neg u_i \vee \neg u_j) \wedge \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq q} (\neg v_i \vee \neg v_j)
\end{equation}
To eliminate invalid phantom cells when $p \times q > n$, phantom clauses forbid vacant coordinate pairs:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{phantom}} = \bigwedge_{r=1}^{p} \bigwedge_{\substack{c=1 \\ (r-1)q + (c-1) \geq n}}^{q} (\neg u_r \vee \neg v_c)
\end{equation}
For $n<6$, the implementation uses pairwise AMO instead of creating grid variables. For $n\geq6$, a true $x_k$ fixes its two coordinates. Coordinate AMO then falsifies every other input through at least one differing coordinate. The encoding is GAC on the original AMO inputs and uses $O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables and $O(n)$ clauses per constraint.

\subsection{Baseline exact formulations}

\subsubsection{Constraint programming formulation (CP)}
The CP model uses $N$ integer variables $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_N\}$. Variable $q_i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ gives the queen's column in row $i$, so the variable indices enforce row occupancy. Three \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints enforce distinct columns and diagonals:
\begin{align}
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 + 1, q_2 + 2, \dots, q_N + N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 - 1, q_2 - 2, \dots, q_N - N)
\end{align}
Régin's matching algorithm is one possible way to enforce GAC for \texttt{AllDifferent} \cite{regin1994filtering}. This model does not establish which propagators or presolve steps either CP solver used in a particular run.

\subsubsection{Integer linear programming formulation (ILP)}
The ILP model uses $N^2$ binary variables $x_{i,j}$, where $x_{i,j}=1$ exactly when cell $(i,j)$ contains a queen. Four families of linear constraints enforce non-attacking placement:
\begin{align}
& \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Row constraints)} \\
& \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Column constraints)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i - j = d}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall d \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\} \quad \text{(Main diagonals)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i + j = s}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall s \in \{2, \dots, 2N\} \quad \text{(Anti-diagonals)}
\end{align}

\subsection{Complexity comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:complexity} gives asymptotic counts for the complete SAT models. An $N\times N$ board has $N$ rows, $N$ columns, and $O(N)$ diagonals, each with at most $N$ cells.

\begin{table}

\caption{Asymptotic size of the N-Queens SAT encodings}
\label{tab:complexity}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllc@{}}
\toprule
Encoding & Total variables & Total clauses & Input GAC via UP \\ \midrule
Binomial (Pairwise) & $N^2$ & $O(N^3)$ & Yes \\
Binary & $N^2 + O(N \log N)$ & $O(N^2 \log N)$ & Yes \\
Commander & $N^2 + O(N^2)$ & $O(N^2)$ & Yes \\

Product & $N^2 + O(N\sqrt{N})$ & $O(N^2)$ & Yes \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
Here, input GAC means that setting one input of an individual AMO constraint to true makes unit propagation falsify its other inputs. It does not imply complete propagation for the full N-Queens formula. For $N\geq2$, let $a_E(k)$ and $c_E(k)$ count auxiliary variables and clauses for one $k$-input AMO, with both zero for $k\leq1$. Exact board-level counts are
\begin{align}
V_E(N)&=N^2+(2N+2)a_E(N)+4\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}a_E(k),\\
C_E(N)&=2N+(2N+2)c_E(N)+4\sum_{k=2}^{N-1}c_E(k).
\end{align}
The $2N$ term counts row and column ALO clauses. The one-constraint counts for pairwise, Binary, and Sequential are
\begin{align}
(a_{\mathrm{pair}},c_{\mathrm{pair}})&=(0,\tbinom{k}{2}),\\
(a_{\mathrm{binary}},c_{\mathrm{binary}})&=(\lceil\log_2 k\rceil,k\lceil\log_2 k\rceil),\\
(a_{\mathrm{seq}},c_{\mathrm{seq}})&=(k-1,3k-4).
\end{align}
Product uses pairwise AMO for $k<6$. For larger $k$, let $p=\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil$ and $q=\lceil k/p\rceil$. Its counts are
\begin{equation}
(a,c)=\left(p+q,\;2k+\binom p2+\binom q2+pq-k\right).
\end{equation}
For Commander, groups have sizes $t_j\leq3$ and $m=\lceil k/3\rceil$. When $k>3$, the recursive counts are
\begin{equation}
a(k)=m+a(m),\qquad c(k)=\sum_j\binom{t_j}{2}+k+m+c(m).
\end{equation}
The pairwise case is the recursion base. The $m$ reverse clauses are included in the count. These expressions count generated clauses before solver preprocessing.

\section{Experimental results and comparison}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
The archived dataset contains 116 aggregate rows across $N\in\{4,8,10,15,20,25,30,40,50,75,100,150,200\}$. The original description identifies a Windows machine with an Intel Core processor and 16~GB of RAM. It omits the exact CPU model, package versions, random seeds, solver thread counts, and individual runs. These gaps limit reproduction and statistical inference. The SAT models use Glucose4 through PySAT \cite{ignatiev2018pysat}. The baselines use CBC through PuLP, Gurobi, CPLEX MIP, OR-Tools CP-SAT, and CPLEX CP Optimizer \cite{mitchell1996lp,gurobi,cplex,ortools}.

The archived CSV reports construction time, solver invocation time, and their sum in seconds. Solution extraction and equivalent postprocessing occur after the solver timer stops, so these totals exclude end-to-end latency. The historical scripts contain both a 120-s, two-repeat configuration and a 300-s, three-repeat configuration. Raw repetitions are unavailable, so the originating protocol of each archived row cannot be recovered. The revised runner records each status, deadline, wall time, solution validation, package version, and worker count. It leaves the archive unchanged and stores new measurements separately.

Solver coverage differs. Only Binary among the five SAT encodings was recorded at $N=150$ and $N=200$; Product ends at $N=50$, while pairwise, Commander, and Sequential end at $N=100$. CBC ends at $N=100$. Gurobi and CP-SAT have recorded results through $N=200$. Missing entries must be read as \emph{not evaluated}, except entries marked with a license symbol. CPLEX MIP Community is limited to 1,000 variables or constraints; its $N=40$ model already has 1,600 binary variables. CP Optimizer Community applies a search-space limit of $2^{1000}$, which prevents the recorded $N=150$ and $N=200$ models from being evaluated under that license. These limits differ from the academic Gurobi license used for the archived Gurobi rows.

\subsection{Propagation and interpretation}
The clauses in Section~3 show that each implemented encoding is GAC on the original inputs of an isolated AMO constraint. This property does not mean that unit propagation alone solves the complete N-Queens formula.

Encoding structure can affect Glucose4's decisions, propagation, conflicts, and learned clauses \cite{marques2009cdcl,audemard2009predicting}. The archived benchmark has no solver-internal counters. The clean-source CSVs record aggregate conflicts, decisions, propagations, and restarts, but not learned-clause LBD distributions or decisions on auxiliary variables. A long implication chain need not span many decision levels, and binary original clauses do not guarantee short learned clauses. The timing data therefore cannot attribute the observed ordering to a specific CDCL mechanism.

Archived figures have unequal method coverage and no run-level uncertainty. The clean-source runs provide raw repetitions at five sizes, but they use one machine and no seeded ablation. Empty-board N-Queens has polynomial constructions, so these timings cannot be generalized to harder completion instances or other combinatorial domains \cite{gent2017complexity}.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
This study compared five established AMO encodings with ILP and CP baselines for empty-board N-Queens. The clean-source runs provide a common-size comparison missing from the archived benchmark. Commander has the lowest SAT median at $N=200$, while Gurobi has the lowest median among the tested methods. Sequential and CBC timed out in all three runs at that size. These findings concern construction-plus-solver-call time on one machine and do not establish general scalability or performance on hard N-Queens variants.

To test whether encoding structure explains the observed SAT ordering, a follow-up should vary Commander implications, Product grid choices, and symmetry breaking under the same protocol. Learned-clause LBD and branching measurements would help distinguish propagation cost from search effects. To test generality, the same comparison should include feasible and infeasible completion instances, additional seeds, and independent machines.
