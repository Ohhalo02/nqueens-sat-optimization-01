\begin{abstract}
The N-Queens problem is a classic combinatorial optimization challenge that has long served as a fundamental benchmark for evaluating problem-solving algorithms and constraint satisfaction strategies. In this paper, we explore the formulation of the N-Queens problem using Boolean Satisfiability (SAT). We implement and systematically compare five distinct encoding schemes for the At-Most-One (AMO) constraint: Binomial, Binary, Commander, Sequential Counter, and Product encodings. Additionally, we conduct a comprehensive comparative analysis of the SAT-based approach against prominent exact methods, including Integer Linear Programming (COIN-OR CBC, Gurobi, and CPLEX MIP) and Constraint Programming (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT and IBM CPLEX CP Optimizer). Our empirical evaluation on instances up to $N=200$ reveals that the Binary encoding achieves superior solving efficiency among SAT formulations by leveraging modern CDCL clause learning, while Sequential Counter generates compact formulas at the cost of auxiliary variable overhead. In comparison with exact methods, commercial Gurobi Optimizer demonstrates elite performance across all scales ($0.60$ seconds at $N=200$) under its academic license. Crucially, among open-source paradigms on massive instances ($N = 150, 200$), the optimized Binary SAT formulation demonstrates extraordinary scalability, solving $N=200$ in just $3.21$ seconds and outperforming Google OR-Tools CP-SAT ($22.49$ seconds) by a factor of seven. Our findings establish that compact CDCL SAT formulations with logarithmic auxiliary structures can substantially surpass specialized global constraint propagation engines in large-scale combinatorial search.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The N-Queens problem is one of the most recognizable puzzles in computer science and mathematics, first proposed by Max Bezzel in 1848 and subsequently studied by prominent mathematicians including Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1850. The challenge involves placing $N$ non-attacking queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard such that no two queens share the same row, column, or diagonal. While seemingly simple, the search space scales exponentially as $N$ grows, rendering naive search strategies completely ineffective for larger instances \cite{bell2009survey}. 

Beyond its recreational origins, the N-Queens problem has established itself as an essential benchmark in the study of Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs), combinatorial optimization, and algorithm design \cite{russell2003artificial}. Its rigid constraints make it an excellent proxy for real-world scenarios such as VLSI testing, parallel memory storage, scheduling, and routing. 

The primary motivation of this study is to systematically evaluate how different problem formulations impact the performance of modern automated reasoning tools. Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) solvers have made tremendous strides in the past two decades, largely driven by Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) architectures \cite{biere2009conflict}. However, compiling high-level combinatorial constraints into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) requires careful consideration. The choice of encoding, particularly for cardinality constraints like "At-Most-One" (AMO), can drastically alter the size of the logical formula and the solver's ability to prune the search space via unit propagation \cite{prestwich2007cnf}.

The contributions of this paper are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We provide a systematic implementation and theoretical comparison of five distinct AMO encoding schemes applied to the N-Queens problem: Binomial (Pairwise), Binary, Commander, Sequential Counter, and Product encodings.
    \item We conduct a rigorous comparative analysis between the optimized SAT approach and prominent exact solvers spanning Integer Linear Programming (PuLP-CBC, Gurobi Optimizer, IBM CPLEX MIP) and Constraint Programming (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, IBM CPLEX CP Optimizer).
    \item We present an extensive empirical evaluation across problem sizes ranging from $N=4$ up to $N=200$, highlighting the scaling characteristics, formula complexity, and runtime limits of each paradigm.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the necessary background on SAT, ILP, and CP. Section 3 details the mathematical formulations and the five proposed SAT encoding schemes. Section 4 presents the experimental setup, empirical results, and a comprehensive comparative analysis with graphical evaluations. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and discusses avenues for future research.

\section{Background \& Related Work}

\subsection{The N-Queens Problem}
Formally, the N-Queens problem seeks a placement of $N$ queens on an $N \times N$ grid such that no two queens threaten each other. This means selecting exactly $N$ cells where no two chosen cells share a row, column, main diagonal, or anti-diagonal. It is a well-known mathematical result that solutions exist for all natural numbers $N \geq 4$ \cite{bell2009survey}. While finding a single valid placement can be done in polynomial time using explicit construction methods, the problem of counting all valid placements (the $N$-Queens Completion problem) is \#P-complete, and finding a solution on a board with pre-placed queens is NP-complete \cite{gent2017complexity}.

\subsection{Boolean Satisfiability (SAT)}
The Boolean Satisfiability problem (SAT) asks whether there exists an assignment of truth values to a set of boolean variables that makes a given logical formula true. Most solvers require the formula to be in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF), a conjunction (AND) of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction (OR) of literals. 

Modern SAT solving relies heavily on variants of the DPLL (Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland) algorithm augmented with CDCL \cite{marques1999grasp, biere2009conflict, moskewicz2001chaff}. State-of-the-art solvers like Glucose \cite{audemard2009predicting, audemard2018glucose} and CaDiCaL utilize aggressive clause learning, adaptive restart policies, Literal Block Distance (LBD) heuristics, and sophisticated variable branching heuristics to solve industrial instances with millions of variables and clauses.

\subsection{Cardinality Constraints}
Many combinatorial problems are expressed using cardinality constraints, which restrict the number of variables in a set $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ that can be true simultaneously. The most critical constraints are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At-Least-One (ALO):} $\sum x_i \geq 1$. In CNF, this is trivially encoded as a single clause: $(x_1 \vee x_2 \vee \dots \vee x_n)$.
    \item \textbf{At-Most-One (AMO):} $\sum x_i \leq 1$. This prevents more than one variable from being true and is the primary bottleneck in translating CSPs to SAT \cite{frisch2005sat}.
    \item \textbf{Exactly-One (EXO):} $\sum x_i = 1$. This is logically equivalent to ALO $\wedge$ AMO.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integer Linear Programming (ILP)}
ILP is a mathematical optimization framework where the objective function and constraints are linear, and decision variables are restricted to integer values \cite{nemhauser1988integer}. Solvers typically utilize LP relaxations combined with Branch and Bound and Branch and Cut techniques. Leading commercial ILP solvers include Gurobi \cite{gurobi} and IBM ILOG CPLEX \cite{cplex}, alongside open-source alternatives like CBC via the PuLP library \cite{mitchell1996lp}.

\subsection{Constraint Programming (CP)}
Constraint Programming focuses on stating constraints over variables and finding solutions satisfying them, typically using systematic search combined with constraint propagation. A key strength of CP is the availability of "global constraints" like \texttt{AllDifferent}, which encapsulate complex subproblems and utilize specialized filtering algorithms (such as Régin's maximum bipartite matching algorithm \cite{regin1994filtering}) to heavily prune domains \cite{rossi2006handbook}. State-of-the-art engines include Google's OR-Tools CP-SAT \cite{ortools} (which hybridizes CP propagation with SAT-based lazy clause generation) and IBM ILOG CPLEX CP Optimizer \cite{cplex}.

\subsection{Related Work}
The translation of CSPs to SAT has been extensively studied. Prestwich \cite{prestwich2007cnf} surveyed various encoding methods. The naive pairwise AMO encoding, while simple, creates $O(n^2)$ clauses, which becomes prohibitive. Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} introduced the sequential counter encoding, achieving arc consistency with $O(n)$ variables and clauses. Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient} proposed the Commander encoding, grouping variables hierarchically. Chen \cite{chen2010new} introduced grid and product-based encodings. Previous studies comparing exact methods often note that while CP generally dominates on N-Queens due to structural awareness, an optimized SAT encoding can remain highly competitive.

\section{Proposed SAT Encodings for N-Queens}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $N$ be the dimension of the board. We define a boolean variable $x_{i,j}$ for each row $i$ and column $j$ ($i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}$). The variable $x_{i,j}$ is TRUE if a queen is placed at row $i$, column $j$, and FALSE otherwise.

The complete N-Queens constraints can be expressed as a single CNF formula:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \Phi^{row}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{row}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{diag}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{anti}_{AMO}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Row and Column Constraints}
Every row must contain exactly one queen (EXO constraint), meaning at least one (ALO) and at most one (AMO).
The ALO constraint for row $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
\bigvee_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j}
\end{equation}
The AMO constraint depends on the chosen encoding scheme. The exact same logic applies to columns. Thus, for rows and columns, the constraint is EXO = ALO $\wedge$ AMO.

\subsection{Diagonal Constraints}
Queens cannot share diagonals. For main diagonals, we define the index $d = i - j$, where $d \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\}$. For anti-diagonals, we define $s = i + j$, where $s \in \{2, \dots, 2N\}$.
Because a diagonal can be empty, we only need to enforce the At-Most-One (AMO) constraint for all cells sharing the same $d$ and $s$. No ALO constraint is required.

\subsection{AMO Encoding Schemes}
Given a set of $n$ Boolean variables $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, the At-Most-One (AMO) constraint specifies that at most one variable in $X$ can evaluate to TRUE:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \leq 1
\end{equation}
Translating this pseudo-Boolean cardinality constraint into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) requires balancing formula size against the pruning strength of unit propagation. We implemented and benchmarked five distinct encoding paradigms.

\subsubsection{Binomial (Pairwise) Encoding}
The classical direct approach forbids every distinct pair of variables from being simultaneously true:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binomial}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
\end{equation}
This formulation introduces zero auxiliary variables but generates $\binom{n}{2} = O(n^2)$ binary clauses per constraint. When applied across all rows, columns, and diagonals of an $N \times N$ chessboard, the total clause count scales as $O(N^3)$. Notably, whenever any variable $x_i$ is assigned TRUE, standard unit propagation immediately forces all other literals $\neg x_j$ ($j \neq i$), thus achieving full Generalized Arc Consistency (GAC) without search.

\subsubsection{Binary Encoding}
The Binary encoding compacts the clause footprint by introducing $m = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ auxiliary Boolean variables $Y = \{y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{m-1}\}$, which function as a shared binary bit-register \cite{frisch2005sat}. Each index $k \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ possesses a unique $m$-bit binary expansion $k = \sum_{b=0}^{m-1} \text{bit}(k, b) \cdot 2^b$. 

We define the bit-literal mapping function $\ell(k, b)$ as:
\begin{equation}
\ell(k, b) = \begin{cases}
y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 1, \\
\neg y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The complete Binary AMO formula is given in closed form by:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binary}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \bigwedge_{b=0}^{m-1} (\neg x_k \vee \ell(k, b))
\end{equation}
If $x_k$ is assigned TRUE, unit propagation forces the register $Y$ to match the unique binary vector of $k$. Any subsequent assignment $x_j = \text{TRUE}$ ($j \neq k$) immediately produces an unsatisfiable clash on at least one bit variable $y_b$. The Binary encoding requires only $O(\log n)$ auxiliary variables and $n \lceil \log_2 n \rceil = O(n \log n)$ 2-literal clauses per constraint ($O(N^2 \log N)$ total clauses across the board).

\subsubsection{Commander Encoding}
Proposed by Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient}, the Commander encoding decomposes the set $X$ recursively. The variables are partitioned into disjoint groups $G_1, \dots, G_m$ of size $g$ (where $g = 3$ is empirically optimal). For each group $G_k$, a new commander variable $c_k$ is instantiated to act as a group representative.

The encoding consists of four complementary sets of clauses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intra-group Pairwise AMO ($\Phi_{\text{intra}}$):} Ensures no two variables within the same group can be simultaneously true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{intra}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{\substack{x_i, x_j \in G_k \\ i < j}} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Forward Implication ($\Phi_{\text{fwd}}$):} Dictates that if any element in $G_k$ is true, commander $c_k$ must be true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{fwd}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{x \in G_k} (\neg x \vee c_k)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Reverse Implication ($\Phi_{\text{rev}}$):} Dictates that commander $c_k$ cannot be true unless at least one variable in $G_k$ is true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{rev}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \left( \neg c_k \vee \bigvee_{x \in G_k} x \right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Commander AMO ($\Phi_{\text{cmd}}$):} Applies AMO recursively across the set of commander variables:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{cmd}} = \text{AMO}(\{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_m\})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The recursion terminates when $n \leq 3$, at which point direct pairwise AMO is applied. The bidirectional equivalence $c_k \leftrightarrow \bigvee_{x \in G_k} x$ established by $\Phi_{\text{fwd}} \wedge \Phi_{\text{rev}}$ is essential: without $\Phi_{\text{rev}}$, commander variables can become unconstrained spurious truths, destroying Generalized Arc Consistency. The complete encoding generates $O(n)$ clauses and $O(n)$ auxiliary variables.

\subsubsection{Sequential Counter Encoding}
Introduced by Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards}, this encoding constructs an accumulator network. It introduces $n-1$ auxiliary variables $s_1, \dots, s_{n-1}$, where $s_i$ signifies the prefix condition $\sum_{j=1}^{i} x_j \geq 1$.

The clauses enforce prefix propagation and collision prohibition:
\begin{align}
& (\neg x_1 \vee s_1) \\
& \bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_i \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg s_{i-1} \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg x_i \vee \neg s_{i-1}) \right) \\
& (\neg x_n \vee \neg s_{n-1})
\end{align}
Sequential Counter introduces $n-1 = O(n)$ auxiliary variables and $3n - 4 = O(n)$ clauses per constraint. It maintains Generalized Arc Consistency via unit propagation: if $x_i = \text{TRUE}$, unit propagation cascades through $s_i \dots s_{n-1}$ to forbid all subsequent $x_j$ ($j > i$), while propagating backward to forbid all antecedent $x_j$ ($j < i$).

\subsubsection{Product Encoding}
The Product encoding decomposes $n$ variables onto a 2-dimensional grid \cite{chen2010new}. We select grid dimensions $p = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ and $q = \lceil n/p \rceil$ such that $n \leq p \times q$. Two sets of auxiliary coordinate variables are introduced: row coordinates $U = \{u_1, \dots, u_p\}$ and column coordinates $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_q\}$.

Each variable $x_k$ ($k \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$) is mapped to grid coordinates $r = \lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1$ and $c = (k \bmod q) + 1$ via channeling clauses:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{chan}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_k \vee u_{\lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1}) \wedge (\neg x_k \vee v_{(k \bmod q) + 1}) \right)
\end{equation}
Pairwise AMO constraints are then enforced independently across $U$ and $V$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{grid}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq p} (\neg u_i \vee \neg u_j) \wedge \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq q} (\neg v_i \vee \neg v_j)
\end{equation}
To eliminate invalid phantom cells when $p \times q > n$, phantom clauses forbid vacant coordinate pairs:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{phantom}} = \bigwedge_{r=1}^{p} \bigwedge_{\substack{c=1 \\ (r-1)q + (c-1) \geq n}}^{q} (\neg u_r \vee \neg v_c)
\end{equation}
\textbf{Threshold Optimization:} For short constraint sets ($n < 6$), especially diagonal slices near the corners of the chessboard, generating the 2D grid overhead is counterproductive. Our implementation dynamically applies direct pairwise AMO for $n < 6$ and activates the 2D product grid only when $n \geq 6$. The Product encoding achieves $O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables and $O(n)$ clauses per constraint.

\subsection{Baseline Exact Formulations}

\subsubsection{Constraint Programming Formulation (CP)}
In Constraint Programming, the N-Queens problem is represented compactly using $N$ integer variables $Q = \{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N\}$, where each variable $q_i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ designates the column position of the queen in row $i$. The row constraints are implicitly satisfied by the variable index structure. The column and diagonal constraints are formulated via three global \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints:
\begin{align}
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 + 1, q_2 + 2, \dots, q_N + N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 - 1, q_2 - 2, \dots, q_N - N)
\end{align}
Rather than expanding constraints into propositional logic, modern CP solvers (e.g., Google OR-Tools CP-SAT and IBM CPLEX CP) propagate \texttt{AllDifferent} globally using Régin's bipartite matching algorithm \cite{regin1994filtering}. This achieves Generalized Arc Consistency in $O(N^{2.5})$ time per filtering cycle.

\subsubsection{Integer Linear Programming Formulation (ILP)}
In Integer Linear Programming, the board is modeled using $N^2$ binary decision variables $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$, where $x_{i,j} = 1$ if and only if a queen occupies cell $(i, j)$. The non-attacking requirements are encoded as four systems of linear equations and inequalities:
\begin{align}
& \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Row constraints)} \\
& \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Column constraints)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i - j = d}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall d \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\} \quad \text{(Main diagonals)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i + j = s}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall s \in \{2, \dots, 2N\} \quad \text{(Anti-diagonals)}
\end{align}

\section{Experimental Results \& Comparative Analysis}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
All computational experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core processor and 16 GB of RAM under 64-bit Windows. The benchmark framework was implemented in Python. 

The solvers evaluated in this study include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SAT Solvers:} The five proposed AMO formulations were compiled into CNF and solved using Glucose4 via the PySAT toolkit \cite{ignatiev2018pysat}.
    \item \textbf{Integer Linear Programming (ILP):} Evaluated using the open-source COIN-OR CBC solver via PuLP \cite{mitchell1996lp}, commercial Gurobi Optimizer 13.0 \cite{gurobi}, and IBM ILOG CPLEX Optimization Studio 22.1 (CPLEX MIP via DOcplex) \cite{cplex}.
    \item \textbf{Constraint Programming (CP):} Evaluated using Google OR-Tools CP-SAT \cite{ortools} and IBM ILOG CPLEX CP Optimizer (CPLEX CP via DOcplex) \cite{cplex}.
\end{itemize}

Experiments were evaluated across board dimensions $N \in \{4, 8, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200\}$. A time limit of 300 seconds was enforced per instance, with runtimes averaged over multiple runs. Commercial Gurobi Optimizer 13.0 was activated using an official academic Web License Service (WLS) license, eliminating variable limits across all benchmark instances up to $N=200$. IBM CPLEX was evaluated under local community edition thresholds ($>2{,}000$ variables/constraints at $N \ge 40$).

\textbf{Analysis:} The empirical results in Table \ref{tab:sat_times} and Figure \ref{fig:performance_comparison} reveal interesting dynamics. While Binomial encoding generates the most clauses, its absence of auxiliary variables enables very fast solving for moderate $N$. Commander encoding with bidirectional clauses achieves exceptional performance at $N=20$ ($0.019$s) and $N=50$ ($0.121$s). At large scale ($N \geq 75$), the Binary encoding emerges as the fastest overall SAT solver, completing $N=100$ in $0.581$s, $N=150$ in $1.587$s, and $N=200$ in just $3.207$s. Conversely, despite its theoretical arc consistency, the Sequential Counter experiences severe degradation at $N=100$ (68.18s) because the vast auxiliary variable space creates a large branching overhead for Glucose4's CDCL heuristic.

\subsection{Discussion: Architectural Drivers of CDCL Performance}
Our empirical findings reveal fundamental interactions between CNF compilation strategies and the internal architecture of Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) engines. We analyze these dynamics across three core CDCL mechanisms:

\subsubsection{VSIDS Branching Distortion and Search Space Bloat}
The Variable State Independent Decaying Sum (VSIDS) heuristic \cite{moskewicz2001chaff} prioritizes decision variables based on their historical participation in conflict resolutions. In cardinality encodings that introduce large numbers of auxiliary variables---such as the Sequential Counter ($O(N^2)$ auxiliary variables) and unoptimized Product grids---the auxiliary literals inadvertently capture a dominant share of conflict activity scores. 

Consequently, VSIDS branches predominantly on auxiliary accumulation states rather than primary queen placement variables ($x_{i,j}$). This distorts the search tree, forcing the solver to explore irrelevant permutations of auxiliary variables that fail to directly resolve board-level geometric clashes. In sharp contrast, the Binomial encoding introduces zero auxiliary variables, and the Binary encoding introduces only $O(N \log N)$ auxiliary bit registers. This allows VSIDS to focus directly on board variables, maintaining high heuristic accuracy throughout search.

\subsubsection{Implication Graph Trail Depth and Clause Generalization}
Under unit propagation, modern solvers maintain a two-watched-literal implication graph. In the Sequential Counter encoding, prefix accumulation relies on linear implication chains: $s_{i-1} \rightarrow s_i$. Propagating a single variable assignment through a line of length $N$ requires traversing $O(N)$ sequential edges on the implication trail. 

When conflicts arise at deep decision levels, the First Unique Implication Point (1-UIP) conflict analysis algorithm derives learned clauses that encompass literals from many intermediate decision levels. These long, multi-level conflict clauses possess weak generalization power, failing to prune substantial subtrees upon backtracking. In contrast, the Binary encoding connects each board variable $x_k$ to auxiliary bits via binary clauses $(\neg x_k \vee \ell(k, b))$. Setting $x_k = \text{TRUE}$ propagates all $m = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ bit variables simultaneously at trail depth 1. This parallel propagation immediately freezes the bit pattern and detects clashes with conflicting variables in a single step, yielding compact, highly general conflict clauses.

\subsubsection{Literal Block Distance (LBD) and Glucose Clause Purging Dynamics}
The Glucose solver \cite{audemard2009predicting, audemard2018glucose} implements an aggressive learned clause database reduction policy governed by Literal Block Distance (LBD)---the number of distinct decision levels appearing in a learned clause. Clauses with $\text{LBD} \leq 2$ (designated as "glue clauses") are deemed highly predictive of future conflicts and are preserved indefinitely in the database. Clauses with high LBD values are periodically purged during database reductions to prevent memory exhaustion and propagation slowdown.

In encodings with deep sequential implication chains (such as Sequential Counter), learned clauses span numerous decision levels, resulting in inflated LBD scores. During periodic clause database cleanups, Glucose systematically purges these clauses, discarding hard-earned conflict knowledge and compelling the solver to re-explore previously traversed conflict subtrees. Conversely, the shallow propagation structure of the Binary encoding produces learned clauses with exceptionally low LBD scores. Glucose recognizes these clauses as high-value glue clauses and retains them permanently, accelerating search velocity and enabling Glucose to solve $N=200$ in just $3.21$ seconds.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This study presented a systematic investigation into Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) formulations for the N-Queens problem and conducted an extensive comparative benchmark against leading Integer Linear Programming (CBC, Gurobi, CPLEX MIP) and Constraint Programming (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT, IBM CPLEX CP) solvers. We evaluated five distinct At-Most-One (AMO) encoding schemes---Binomial, Binary, Commander with bidirectional GAC preservation, Sequential Counter, and Product with threshold optimization---across board sizes from $N=4$ to $N=200$.

The empirical findings challenge conventional assumptions in combinatorial problem solving. Commercial Gurobi Optimizer demonstrates exceptional performance across all scales ($0.60$s at $N=200$) when unlocked with an academic license. Crucially, among open-source solvers where Constraint Programming systems with global \texttt{AllDifferent} filtering (Google CP-SAT) were historically viewed as the most scalable approach, our results demonstrate that an optimized Binary SAT encoding running on Glucose4 outperforms Google CP-SAT by a factor of $7.0\times$ to $9.4\times$ on massive instances ($1.59$s vs. $14.95$s at $N=150$, and $3.21$s vs. $22.49$s at $N=200$). Through detailed analysis of CDCL mechanics, we demonstrated that the logarithmic bit-counter structure of Binary encoding minimizes VSIDS branching distortion, suppresses implication trail depth, and generates low-LBD glue clauses that avoid clause database purging.

Future research will investigate: (1) integrating symmetry-breaking predicates to exploit the dihedral group $D_4$ symmetries of the chessboard, (2) tailoring dynamic restart strategies to binary bit-register encodings, (3) evaluating parallel portfolio SAT solvers on ultra-scale instances ($N \geq 500$), and (4) transferring these logarithmic encoding principles to related combinatorial problems such as graph coloring, tournament scheduling, and circuit placement.
