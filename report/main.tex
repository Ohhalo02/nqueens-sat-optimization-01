\begin{abstract}
This paper compares five at-most-one (AMO) encodings for Boolean satisfiability formulations of empty-board N-Queens: pairwise, binary, commander, sequential counter, and product. The same placement constraints are modeled with integer linear programming (CBC, Gurobi, and CPLEX MIP) and constraint programming (OR-Tools CP-SAT and CPLEX CP Optimizer). An archived benchmark covers $N=4$ to $N=200$ with unequal solver coverage and incomplete provenance. Two clean-source runs evaluate all five SAT encodings, CBC, Gurobi, and CP-SAT at $N\in\{20,50,100,150,200\}$, with three repetitions, one worker, and a 120-s process deadline per run. At $N=200$, the observed median construction-plus-solver-call times are 2.252~s for Commander, 2.806~s for Product, 3.208~s for Binary, 0.421~s for Gurobi, and 44.776~s for CP-SAT; Sequential and CBC time out in all three repetitions. These times exclude solution extraction and process startup, which are recorded separately. Exact formula-size checks and unit-propagation tests confirm that each implemented AMO encoding enforces the intended constraint and propagates a selected input to the falsity of all others. The datasets are kept separate, and the results do not imply a general ranking beyond the evaluated empty-board instances.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The N-Queens problem asks for $N$ non-attacking queens on an $N\times N$ board. A valid placement exists for $N=1$ and every $N\geq4$, and explicit constructions find one in polynomial time. Nevertheless, the problem provides a structured comparison of modeling and implementation overhead across exact solvers. Its easy decision version should not be confused with the harder N-Queens Completion variant \cite{bell2009survey,gent2017complexity}.

N-Queens is commonly used to illustrate constraint satisfaction and compare formulations. Results on an empty board do not, by themselves, predict performance on arbitrary scheduling, routing, or other combinatorial problems \cite{gent2017complexity}.

The primary motivation of this study is to systematically evaluate how different problem formulations impact the performance of modern automated reasoning tools. Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) solvers have made tremendous strides in the past two decades, largely driven by Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) architectures \cite{marques2009cdcl}. However, compiling high-level combinatorial constraints into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) requires careful consideration. The choice of encoding, particularly for cardinality constraints like ``At-Most-One'' (AMO), can drastically alter the size of the logical formula and the solver's ability to prune the search space via unit propagation \cite{prestwich2007cnf}.

The contributions are (i) a common implementation and exact size analysis of five established AMO encodings, (ii) an archived ILP/CP comparison and a separately recorded, common-protocol large-board comparison, and (iii) an explicit account of coverage, timing, and license limitations. No new SAT encoding or general-purpose optimization algorithm is claimed.

Section 2 reviews the background. Section 3 specifies the formulations and encodings. Section 4 reports the archived and controlled measurements with their limitations. Section 5 concludes.

\section{Background \& Related Work}

\subsection{The N-Queens Problem}
A solution places exactly one queen in each row and column and at most one on each diagonal. The empty-board decision problem has constructive solutions for $N=1$ and $N\geq4$; $N=2,3$ are infeasible. In contrast, the decision and counting versions of N-Queens Completion, where some queens are pre-placed, are NP-complete and \#P-complete, respectively \cite{gent2017complexity}. The experiments below concern one feasible placement on an empty board, not completion or counting.

\subsection{Boolean Satisfiability (SAT)}
The Boolean Satisfiability problem (SAT) asks whether there exists an assignment of truth values to a set of boolean variables that makes a given logical formula true. Most solvers require the formula to be in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF), a conjunction (AND) of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction (OR) of literals. 

Modern SAT solving relies heavily on variants of the DPLL (Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland) algorithm augmented with CDCL \cite{marques1999grasp, marques2009cdcl, moskewicz2001chaff}. Glucose employs clause learning and evaluates learned clauses using literal block distance (LBD), the number of distinct decision levels appearing in a learned clause \cite{audemard2009predicting,audemard2018glucose}. The present benchmark uses Glucose4 through PySAT.

\subsection{Cardinality Constraints}
Many combinatorial problems are expressed using cardinality constraints, which restrict the number of variables in a set $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ that can be true simultaneously. The most critical constraints are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At-Least-One (ALO):} $\sum x_i \geq 1$. In CNF, this is trivially encoded as a single clause: $(x_1 \vee x_2 \vee \dots \vee x_n)$.
    \item \textbf{At-Most-One (AMO):} $\sum x_i \leq 1$. This prevents more than one variable from being true and is the primary bottleneck in translating CSPs to SAT \cite{frisch2005sat}.
    \item \textbf{Exactly-One (EXO):} $\sum x_i = 1$. This is logically equivalent to ALO $\wedge$ AMO.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Integer Linear Programming (ILP)}
ILP is a mathematical optimization framework where the objective function and constraints are linear, and decision variables are restricted to integer values \cite{nemhauser1988integer}. Solvers typically utilize LP relaxations combined with Branch and Bound and Branch and Cut techniques. Leading commercial ILP solvers include Gurobi \cite{gurobi} and IBM ILOG CPLEX \cite{cplex}, alongside open-source alternatives like CBC via the PuLP library \cite{mitchell1996lp}.

\subsection{Constraint Programming (CP)}
Constraint Programming focuses on stating constraints over variables and finding solutions satisfying them, typically using systematic search combined with constraint propagation. A key strength of CP is the availability of ``global constraints'' like \texttt{AllDifferent}, which encapsulate complex subproblems and utilize specialized filtering algorithms (such as Régin's maximum bipartite matching algorithm \cite{regin1994filtering}) to heavily prune domains \cite{rossi2006handbook}. The evaluated engines are Google's hybrid CP-SAT solver \cite{ortools} and IBM ILOG CPLEX CP Optimizer \cite{cplex}. Their internal propagators and search policies should not be inferred from the high-level model alone.

\subsection{Related Work}
The translation of CSPs to SAT has been extensively studied. Prestwich \cite{prestwich2007cnf} surveyed various encoding methods. The naive pairwise AMO encoding, while simple, creates $O(n^2)$ clauses, which becomes prohibitive. Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} introduced the sequential counter encoding, achieving arc consistency with $O(n)$ variables and clauses. Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient} proposed the Commander encoding, grouping variables hierarchically. Chen \cite{chen2010new} introduced grid and product-based encodings. The five encodings compared here are established methods. The contribution is an empirical comparison for this implementation and dataset rather than a claim of a new encoding.

\section{SAT Encodings for N-Queens}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $N$ be the dimension of the board. A Boolean variable $x_{i,j}$ is defined for each row $i$ and column $j$ ($i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}$). The variable $x_{i,j}$ is TRUE if a queen is placed at row $i$, column $j$, and FALSE otherwise.

The board-level constraint has six components. For each encoding $E$, auxiliary variables $Y$ are allocated separately for each AMO set, and the resulting CNF $\Phi_E(X,Y)$ satisfies $\Phi_{\mathrm{board}}(X)\iff\exists Y\,\Phi_E(X,Y)$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi = \Phi^{row}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{row}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{ALO} \wedge \Phi^{col}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{diag}_{AMO} \wedge \Phi^{anti}_{AMO}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Row and Column Constraints}
Every row must contain exactly one queen (EXO constraint), meaning at least one (ALO) and at most one (AMO).
The ALO constraint for row $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
\bigvee_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j}
\end{equation}
The AMO constraint depends on the chosen encoding scheme. The exact same logic applies to columns. Thus, for rows and columns, the constraint is EXO = ALO $\wedge$ AMO.

\subsection{Diagonal Constraints}
Queens cannot share diagonals. For main diagonals, the index is defined as $d = i - j$, where $d \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\}$. For anti-diagonals, the index is defined as $s = i + j$, where $s \in \{2, \dots, 2N\}$.
Because a diagonal can be empty, only the At-Most-One (AMO) constraint must be enforced for all cells sharing the same $d$ and $s$. No ALO constraint is required.

\subsection{AMO Encoding Schemes}
Given a set of $n$ Boolean variables $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, the At-Most-One (AMO) constraint specifies that at most one variable in $X$ can evaluate to TRUE:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \leq 1
\end{equation}
Translating this pseudo-Boolean cardinality constraint into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) requires balancing formula size against the pruning strength of unit propagation. Five distinct encoding paradigms are implemented and benchmarked in this work.

\subsubsection{Binomial (Pairwise) Encoding}
The classical direct approach forbids every distinct pair of variables from being simultaneously true:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binomial}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
\end{equation}
This formulation introduces zero auxiliary variables but generates $\binom{n}{2} = O(n^2)$ binary clauses per constraint. When applied across all rows, columns, and diagonals of an $N \times N$ chessboard, the total clause count scales as $O(N^3)$. Notably, whenever any variable $x_i$ is assigned TRUE, standard unit propagation immediately forces all other literals $\neg x_j$ ($j \neq i$), thus achieving full Generalized Arc Consistency (GAC) without search.

\subsubsection{Binary Encoding}
The Binary encoding compacts the clause footprint by introducing $m = \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ auxiliary Boolean variables $Y = \{y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{m-1}\}$, which function as a shared binary bit-register \cite{frisch2005sat}. Each index $k \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$ possesses a unique $m$-bit binary expansion $k = \sum_{b=0}^{m-1} \text{bit}(k, b) \cdot 2^b$. 

The bit-literal mapping function $\ell(k, b)$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\ell(k, b) = \begin{cases}
y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 1, \\
\neg y_b & \text{if bit } b \text{ of } k \text{ is } 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The complete Binary AMO formula is given in closed form by:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{binary}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \bigwedge_{b=0}^{m-1} (\neg x_k \vee \ell(k, b))
\end{equation}
If $x_k$ is true, unit propagation fixes every bit in $Y$. For every $j\ne k$, at least one required bit differs, and the corresponding binary clause immediately propagates $\neg x_j$. Thus this implementation is generalized arc consistent (GAC) with respect to the original AMO inputs; no claim is made about propagation completeness over arbitrary auxiliary assignments. It uses $\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ auxiliary variables and $n\lceil\log_2n\rceil$ binary clauses per constraint.

\subsubsection{Commander Encoding}
Proposed by Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007efficient}, the Commander encoding decomposes the set $X$ recursively. The variables are partitioned into disjoint groups $G_1, \dots, G_m$ of at most $g=3$ elements, following the implementation. For each group $G_k$, a new commander variable $c_k$ is instantiated to act as a group representative.

The encoding consists of four complementary sets of clauses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intra-group Pairwise AMO ($\Phi_{\text{intra}}$):} Ensures no two variables within the same group can be simultaneously true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{intra}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{\substack{x_i, x_j \in G_k \\ i < j}} (\neg x_i \vee \neg x_j)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Forward Implication ($\Phi_{\text{fwd}}$):} Dictates that if any element in $G_k$ is true, commander $c_k$ must be true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{fwd}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \bigwedge_{x \in G_k} (\neg x \vee c_k)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Reverse Implication ($\Phi_{\text{rev}}$):} Dictates that commander $c_k$ cannot be true unless at least one variable in $G_k$ is true:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{rev}} = \bigwedge_{k=1}^{m} \left( \neg c_k \vee \bigvee_{x \in G_k} x \right)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Commander AMO ($\Phi_{\text{cmd}}$):} Applies AMO recursively across the set of commander variables:
    \begin{equation}
    \Phi_{\text{cmd}} = \text{AMO}(\{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_m\})
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The recursion terminates at $n\leq3$ with pairwise AMO. The implementation includes reverse implications, so $c_k\leftrightarrow\bigvee_{x\in G_k}x$. The reverse clauses are optional for the projected AMO relation and for GAC on its input variables; forward implications, intra-group AMO, and recursive commander AMO already propagate $\neg x_j$ after one input $x_i$ becomes true \cite{klieber2007efficient}. The implemented version uses $O(n)$ auxiliary variables and clauses.

\subsubsection{Sequential Counter Encoding}
Introduced by Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards}, this encoding constructs an accumulator network. It introduces $n-1$ auxiliary variables $s_1, \dots, s_{n-1}$, where $s_i$ carries one-way prefix implications needed to detect a second true input; the clauses do not require $s_i$ to equal the prefix disjunction under every satisfying auxiliary assignment.

The clauses enforce prefix propagation and collision prohibition:
\begin{align}
& (\neg x_1 \vee s_1) \\
& \bigwedge_{i=2}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_i \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg s_{i-1} \vee s_i) \wedge (\neg x_i \vee \neg s_{i-1}) \right) \\
& (\neg x_n \vee \neg s_{n-1})
\end{align}
Sequential Counter introduces $n-1 = O(n)$ auxiliary variables and $3n - 4 = O(n)$ clauses per constraint. It maintains Generalized Arc Consistency via unit propagation: if $x_i = \text{TRUE}$, unit propagation cascades through $s_i \dots s_{n-1}$ to forbid all subsequent $x_j$ ($j > i$), while propagating backward to forbid all antecedent $x_j$ ($j < i$).

\subsubsection{Product Encoding}
The Product encoding decomposes $n$ variables onto a 2-dimensional grid \cite{chen2010new}. Grid dimensions $p = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$ and $q = \lceil n/p \rceil$ are selected such that $n \leq p \times q$. Two sets of auxiliary coordinate variables are introduced: row coordinates $U = \{u_1, \dots, u_p\}$ and column coordinates $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_q\}$.

Each variable $x_k$ ($k \in \{0, \dots, n-1\}$) is mapped to grid coordinates $r = \lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1$ and $c = (k \bmod q) + 1$ via channeling clauses:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{chan}} = \bigwedge_{k=0}^{n-1} \left( (\neg x_k \vee u_{\lfloor k/q \rfloor + 1}) \wedge (\neg x_k \vee v_{(k \bmod q) + 1}) \right)
\end{equation}
Pairwise AMO constraints are then enforced independently across $U$ and $V$:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{grid}} = \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq p} (\neg u_i \vee \neg u_j) \wedge \bigwedge_{1 \leq i < j \leq q} (\neg v_i \vee \neg v_j)
\end{equation}
To eliminate invalid phantom cells when $p \times q > n$, phantom clauses forbid vacant coordinate pairs:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\text{phantom}} = \bigwedge_{r=1}^{p} \bigwedge_{\substack{c=1 \\ (r-1)q + (c-1) \geq n}}^{q} (\neg u_r \vee \neg v_c)
\end{equation}
\textbf{Threshold Optimization:} For short constraint sets ($n < 6$), especially diagonal slices near the corners of the chessboard, generating the 2D grid overhead is counterproductive. The implementation dynamically applies direct pairwise AMO for $n < 6$ and activates the 2D product grid only when $n \geq 6$. If $x_k$ is true, its two coordinate variables become true. Coordinate AMO then falsifies every other coordinate, and each other input differs on at least one coordinate; unit propagation therefore falsifies every other input. The implementation is GAC on original AMO inputs and uses $O(\sqrt{n})$ auxiliary variables and $O(n)$ clauses per constraint.

\subsection{Baseline Exact Formulations}

\subsubsection{Constraint Programming Formulation (CP)}
In Constraint Programming, the N-Queens problem is represented compactly using $N$ integer variables $Q = \{q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N\}$, where each variable $q_i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ designates the column position of the queen in row $i$. The row constraints are implicitly satisfied by the variable index structure. The column and diagonal constraints are formulated via three global \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints:
\begin{align}
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 + 1, q_2 + 2, \dots, q_N + N) \\
& \text{AllDifferent}(q_1 - 1, q_2 - 2, \dots, q_N - N)
\end{align}
The model supplies three \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints. Régin's matching algorithm is one possible generalized arc-consistency method \cite{regin1994filtering}; the actual propagation depends on the solver, its version, and presolve settings. In particular, CP-SAT may combine Boolean decompositions, bounds propagation, and matching-based filtering \cite{ortools}. No filtering complexity for a specific run is inferred from this model description.

\subsubsection{Integer Linear Programming Formulation (ILP)}
In Integer Linear Programming, the board is modeled using $N^2$ binary decision variables $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$, where $x_{i,j} = 1$ if and only if a queen occupies cell $(i, j)$. The non-attacking requirements are encoded as four systems of linear equations and inequalities:
\begin{align}
& \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Row constraints)} \\
& \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, N\} \quad \text{(Column constraints)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i - j = d}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall d \in \{-(N-1), \dots, N-1\} \quad \text{(Main diagonals)} \\
& \sum_{\substack{1 \leq i, j \leq N \\ i + j = s}} x_{i,j} \leq 1 \quad \forall s \in \{2, \dots, 2N\} \quad \text{(Anti-diagonals)}
\end{align}

(a_{\mathrm{pair}},c_{\mathrm{pair}})&=(0,\tbinom{k}{2}),\\

\begin{equation}
(a,c)=\left(p+q,\;2k+\binom p2+\binom q2+pq-k\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
a(k)=m+a(m),\qquad c(k)=\sum_j\binom{t_j}{2}+k+m+c(m).
\end{equation}
The pairwise case is the recursion base. The $m$ reverse clauses are included in the count. These expressions count generated clauses before solver preprocessing.

\section{Experimental Results \& Comparative Analysis}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
The archived dataset contains 116 aggregate rows across $N\in\{4,8,10,15,20,25,30,40,50,75,100,150,200\}$. It was collected on a Windows machine described in the original experiment as having an Intel Core processor and 16~GB of RAM; the exact CPU model, package versions at collection time, random seeds, solver thread counts, and per-run measurements were not retained. These omissions limit independent reproduction and statistical inference. The five SAT formulations use Glucose4 through PySAT \cite{ignatiev2018pysat}; the comparison models use CBC through PuLP, Gurobi, CPLEX MIP, OR-Tools CP-SAT, and CPLEX CP Optimizer \cite{mitchell1996lp,gurobi,cplex,ortools}.

The archived CSV reports construction time, solver invocation time, and their sum in seconds. SAT solution extraction was performed after the solver timer stopped; equivalent postprocessing was also outside the reported total for the other models. Therefore, the displayed times are \emph{not} end-to-end latencies. The historical scripts contain both a 120-s, two-repeat configuration and a 300-s, three-repeat configuration, and raw repetitions are unavailable. The exact originating protocol for each archived row cannot be recovered. The revised runner records per-repeat status, full-process deadlines, wall time, validation, package versions, and worker count without overwriting this archive; its outputs are kept separate from the historical tables and figures below.

Solver coverage differs. Only Binary among the five SAT encodings was recorded at $N=150$ and $N=200$; Product ends at $N=50$, while pairwise, Commander, and Sequential end at $N=100$. CBC ends at $N=100$. Gurobi and CP-SAT have recorded results through $N=200$. Missing entries must be read as \emph{not evaluated}, except entries marked with a license symbol. CPLEX MIP Community is limited to 1,000 variables or constraints; its $N=40$ model already has 1,600 binary variables. CP Optimizer Community applies a search-space limit of $2^{1000}$, which prevents the recorded $N=150$ and $N=200$ models from being evaluated under that license. These limits differ from the academic Gurobi license used for the archived Gurobi rows.

\subsection{Propagation and Interpretation of the Measurements}
For an isolated AMO constraint, pairwise clauses immediately falsify all other inputs after one input becomes true. The Binary encoding fixes a unique auxiliary bit pattern, and every other input disagrees with at least one bit. The Product encoding fixes one row and one column coordinate and eliminates every other input through coordinate AMO. Commander and Sequential propagate the same input consequence through their respective hierarchical and prefix structures. Thus all five implemented encodings are GAC on the original inputs of one AMO constraint. This statement does not imply that unit propagation alone solves the complete N-Queens formula.

The structural differences could affect Glucose4's branching, propagation, conflicts, and learned clauses \cite{marques2009cdcl,audemard2009predicting}. The archive has no solver-internal counters; the clean-source raw data record aggregate conflicts, decisions, propagations, and restarts for completed SAT runs, but not decisions on auxiliary variables, learned-clause LBD distributions, deletion events, or controlled ablations. In particular, a long sequential implication chain can be traversed within one decision level; its length does not imply high LBD. Likewise, binary original clauses do not guarantee short learned clauses. The observed runtime ordering is therefore reported without attributing it to VSIDS distortion or low-LBD ``glue'' clauses.

The historical plots have unequal solver coverage and no raw repetition-level uncertainty. The two clean-source runs supply raw repetitions for five common sizes but no independent machine or seeded ablation. The N-Queens empty-board feasibility problem has known polynomial constructions, so trends from this instance family should not be generalized to hard completion instances or other combinatorial domains \cite{gent2017complexity}. A broader follow-up should fix seeds where supported, use additional machines, and include both SAT and UNSAT completion instances.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This study compared five established SAT AMO encodings and ILP/CP baselines for finding one placement on an empty N-Queens board. Formula sizes follow the stated construction, and the archived tables agree with the CSV to the displayed precision. In the archive, Binary/Glucose4 records 3.207~s at $N=200$, compared with 22.487~s for CP-SAT and 0.601~s for Gurobi; no other SAT encoding was recorded at that size. The two clean-source runs cover all five SAT encodings at $N\in\{20,50,100,150,200\}$. At $N=200$, Commander, Product, and Binary record medians of 2.252, 2.806, and 3.208~s, respectively, while Sequential and CBC time out in all three attempts. These results correct the coverage gap but do not establish a general scalability claim. The construction-plus-solver-call metric excludes solution extraction, and the historical run-level provenance remains incomplete.

Further work should compare Commander with and without reverse implications, test Product threshold and phantom-clause choices, and evaluate a valid symmetry-breaking constraint under the same protocol for every paradigm. Solver-level counts of conflicts, decisions, propagations, and learned-clause LBD would permit the proposed CDCL explanations to be tested. Harder N-Queens Completion or blocked-board families, including infeasible instances, would broaden the evidence beyond empty-board feasibility.
